Approach & Sources

A paired-site design

Invaded and uninvaded sites burnt in the same fire event — so any difference in recovery is attributable to Lantana, not to how hard the fire hit. Observational, not experimental; the paired comparison controls for confounds.

Emerging Picture

Key findings

Reading the difference maps and recovery curves across the pairs, two opposite signals appear in the invaded sites.

↑ Understorey

Fast regreening after fire — but driven by Lantana dominating the understorey, not by native regeneration. High greenness can mask a shift in what is growing back.

↓ Overstorey

Slower canopy / overstorey recovery where Lantana is present — consistent with invaded sites not converging with uninvaded references, even over the monitored window.

Assumption: darker NDVI signal ≈ overstorey growth. Signals are directional for an in-progress paired analysis, not final causal claims.

Scientific Context

References

Lantana's fire-risk effects are documented; whether it measurably slows recovery rate — detectable via NDVI, against uninvaded sites in the same fire — has not been directly tested. That is the gap.

Berry et al. 2011 — Lantana builds dense, continuous fuel beds, altering fire intensity/spread in landscapes that wouldn't otherwise carry fire.
Rossiter-Rachor et al. 2008; Setterfield et al. 2010 — Northern-Australia parallel: invasive gamba grass amplifies fuel loads and fire intensity.
Gooden et al. 2009 — Post-fire, Lantana-invaded sites do not converge with uninvaded reference sites, even after removal.
McColl-Gausden et al. 2023 — Lantana abundance higher at high-severity 2019–20 fire sites.

Evidence to inform fire management, weed-control priorities and native restoration in Lantana-affected eastern Australia.

Recovery isn't fixed. If Lantana measurably slows canopy recovery, that justifies targeting weed control to specific areas — and because NDVI is scalable, the approach implements across a much larger area than field survey alone.
